# NEG-001 · Identity drift over a long multi-character take
**Farmed 2026-08-13. The studio's first paid learning on a Thistlewood's render.**

## What was fired
minimax H3 · 768P · **15s** · one keyframe (KF-A1.1) · prompt 2,553 chars ·
**four beats in sequence** · **five characters** · camera pushing in throughout.

Beats requested: unwrap and wind → cylinder turns → four children arrive →
Richard ties the apron and smooths the front.

## Verdict
**FAILED. Julian: "we lost the characters and it just didn't work at all."**
Character identity degraded. Once identity goes, the take is unusable no matter
how well the beats landed — so beat delivery could not even be assessed.

## What this does and does not prove
It does NOT prove that scene-level keyframes are wrong, and it does NOT
vindicate a keyframe per beat. The controlling variable is neither.

The comparison that matters: the MP.B1 take earlier the same day was **12s with
the same five characters** and was judged the better of two providers. Same
route, same cast, same style. The difference is that MP.B1 asked the characters
to hold near-static business, while this asked all five to act in sequence with
the camera travelling throughout.

**Working hypothesis: MOTION BUDGET, not duration and not beat count.** Identity
survives in proportion to how much simultaneous character motion a take demands.
Five people acting in sequence over fifteen seconds exceeds it.

## Unknown, and worth one cheap render to find
Where the ceiling actually sits. The next test should change ONE variable:
same keyframe, same route, **8 seconds, ONE beat, one character moving** — Tom
unwraps and winds while nobody else moves and the camera holds still. If
identity holds, the constraint is motion, not time, and takes get planned by
how many people move rather than by how many seconds run.

## Standing consequence until measured
Do not fire a take asking more than two characters to act in sequence, and do
not combine that with a travelling camera. Where a scene needs four beats, cut
it — and prefer the cut that puts the most important gesture at the START of its
own take rather than the end of a crowded one. The apron was last here, which is
the worst place for the series' defining gesture.
